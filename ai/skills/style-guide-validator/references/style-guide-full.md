# Critical Manufacturing MES Documentation Style Guide

Use this guide to keep Critical Manufacturing documentation clear, consistent, and easy to validate.

If following a rule reduces clarity in a specific case, use judgment and prefer the clearer wording.

## Capitalization

What follows is a list of best practices for using capitalization.

### Capitalize

!!! success "Capitalize"
    - The first letter of a sentence or phrase.
    - All proper nouns.
    - Critical Manufacturing MES GUI elements.
    - Critical Manufacturing MES business elements.
    - All first letters of headers and titles.
    - Brand, product, or service names.
    - Protocol, device, and port names.
    - All words joined by a forward slash, if the first word is capitalized.

### Do not capitalize

!!! failure "Do not capitalize"
    - Common nouns.
    - Prepositions of four or fewer letters in titles.
    - Whole words for emphasis.
    - Critical Manufacturing MES operations when used as verbs.
    - Words like:
        - internet
        - web
        - website
        - email
        - online

Remember that if there is more than one of something, it is a common noun (customer versus Customer) and if you are not sure use lowercase as your default. As a general guideline, capitalize and spell screen element names to match their appearance in the GUI.

## Common Mistakes

| **Guideline** | **Why** | **Do** | **Don't** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Misuse of articles a and an** | Using a or an depends on the pronunciation (of the word that follows it) and on whether you pronounce an acronym like a word or pronounce each letter. You use an when the word that follows the article begins with a vowel sound, even if it is written with a consonant. You use a when the word following this article begins with a consonant or u sound. When you are not sure, refer to an online dictionary that enables you to hear the word. | The upgrade takes an hour. Open the collapsed panel to show Spark worker identifiers and a hyperlink to access Spark worker logs. An MES (em, ee, ess). | The upgrade takes a hour. Open the collapsed panel to show Spark worker identifiers and an hyperlink to access Spark worker logs. A MES (em, ee, ess). |
| **It's versus its** | It's is a contraction, it is only used when short for it is. Its is a possessive, used to indicate that something belongs to something (used when not talking about a person). | It's essential that you create a Facility before creating an Area. When viewing a Material, its sections are on the right. | Its essential that you create a Facility before creating an Area. When viewing a Material, it's sections are on the right. |
| **Let's versus lets** | Let's is the contracted form of let us. Lets is the third-person singular form of the verb let, which means to allow or permit. | Let's celebrate the most wonderful time of the year together. Critical Manufacturing MES lets factories become more organized. | Lets celebrate the most wonderful time of the year together. Critical Manufacturing MES let's factories become more organized. |
| **You're versus your** | You're is a contraction of you are. Your is a possessive, something that belongs to you. | When your Material is ready, track it in. When you're done with the Checklist, select Close. | When you're Material is ready, track it in. When your done with the Checklist, select Close. |
| **Number,No. and no.** | The correct way to abbreviate number is No. or no. (as needed). | Apply the exact no. of Materials. No. 507 is the Transfer Order you need. | Apply the exact nr of Materials. Nº 507 is the Transfer Order you need. |
| **This versus these** | Use this when referring to singular nouns and these for plural nouns. | This Flow needs to have Steps associated to it. These Steps need to be associated to a Flow. | These Flow needs to have Steps associated to it. This Steps need to be associated to a Flow. |
| **Among versus between** | Among is used to refer to things with no clear separation because they are part of a group of things. The word between is used to refer to two or more things that are clearly separated. | Among the available options is the IoT Schema. Configure Critical Manufacturing MES to ship Materials between two Facilities. | Between the available options is the IoT Schema. Configure Critical Manufacturing MES to ship Materials among two facilities. |
| **Fewer versus less** | Fewer refers to quantifiable items, items that you can count individually. Less refers to non-quantifiable items, that is, items that you can't count individually. | Ten Business Data entities or fewer. X must be less or equal. | Ten Business Data entities or less. X must be fewer or equal. |
| **Many versus much** | Many is used to describe large quantities of a countable item. Much is used to describe large quantities of an uncountable item. | You have many options in the Material page. This service makes the calls much more efficient. | You have much options in the Material page. This service makes the calls many more efficient. |
| **Compared to versus compared with** | Both prepositions, to and with, can be used after compare. However, American English prefers the use of compared to. | An activity is compared to the previous Maintenance Plan version only by its name. | An activity is compared with the previous Maintenance Plan version only by its name. |
| **Associated with versus associated to** | The correct phrasal verb structure is associated with. Associated to is occasionally acceptable when speaking about certain IT concepts, but in general associated with is preferable nearly every time. Associated to is non-standard English. | Area is Active and has no other identical Maintenance Plan currently associated with it. | All Transfer Requirements associated to the MAO will be listed in this section. |
| **Misplacement of only** | The adverb only should be placed as close as possible to the word it modifies in a sentence, so that the meaning of the sentence is clear. | You can select only one entry from the list. (Meaning: one entry can be selected, no more. ) | You can only select one entry from the list. (Meaning: you can only select it, you can't do anything else with it. ) |
| **Wrong verb form following the auxiliary verb do** | When the auxiliary verb do is in the past tense, the main verb gets the bare infinite. | The Facility did have many workstations. | The Facility did had many workstations. |
| **Lose versus loose** | The spelling is similar, but the meaning is different. Lose is a verb that means unable to find something. Loose is an adjective that means not tightly attached, fastened. | The Material loses the Sub-Material. The components are loosely coupled. | The Material looses the Sub-Material. The components are losely coupled. |
| **Irregular plurals** | Beware of words that do not require the s to form the plural. Consider the following: Life - Lives<br/>Child - Children<br/>Analysis - Analyses<br/>Information - this is an uncountable noun, there is no plural form | Business Intelligence tools provide various analyses. | Business Intelligence tools provide various analysis. |
| **Sign in/Log in versus sign-in/login** | Sign in/Log in is the verb form. Sign-in/Login is the noun form. | You can sign in/log in using the sign-in/login screen. | You can sign-in/login using the sign in/log in screen. |
| **Misspellings/Typos** | There is a group of words that are commonly misspelled. See the examples provided. | below<br/>across<br/>environment<br/>forward<br/>occurred<br/>occurring<br/>which | bellow<br/>accross<br/>enviroment<br/>foward<br/>occured<br/>ocuring<br/>wich |
| **I versus i** | I is always capitalized no matter where it comes in a sentence. | I saw Critical Manufacturing MES for the first time in August and I was amazed. | I saw Critical Manufacturing MES for the first time in August and i was amazed. |
| **12:00 AM versus 12:00 PM** | 12:00 AM = 00:00 H = Midnight<br/>12:00 PM = 12:00 H = Noon/Midday<br/>To avoid confusion, prefer the use of midnight and noon/midday. | The meeting took place at noon. The version update starts at midnight. | The meeting took place at 12:00 AM. The version update starts at 12:00 PM. |
| **ID versus Id/id** | Although you will see all of these being used, try to be consistent and prefer the use of ID. | The ID of the device is printed on the box. | The id of the device is printed on the box. |
| **Advice versus advise** | Advice is a noun that means the giving of an opinion or recommendation as a guide to action, or conduct. Advise is a verb that means to give counsel, offer an opinion, or suggestion. | Critical Manufacturing advises you to use quality hardware. This is good advice for those who are starting their business. | Critical Manufacturing advices you to use quality hardware. This is good advise for those who are starting their business. |
| **May versus might** | Even though these are generally interchangeable, there are cases when one does work better than the other. When you are expressing something that is possible, factual or could well be factual, may is what you want. When something is quite unlikely to happen, when it is a hypothetical scenario, then use might. | Data may come from different sources and in different ways and be manipulated at almost every stage of your project. | Data might come from different sources and in different ways and be manipulated at almost every stage of your project. |
| **Once versus after** | Once is not a synonym for after. | After you save the document, you can close the app. | Once you save the document, you can close the app. |
| **Since versus because** | Whenever possible, choose words that have one clear meaning. | Because you created the table, you can change it. | Since you created the table, you can change it. |
| **Misuse of that and which** | That and which are not always interchangeable. Which introduces clauses that are non-restrictive, meaning that the sentence without the clause would work as a sentence just the same. That introduces a restrictive clause, meaning that the clause is essential for the whole sentence. Note: Which is usually preceded by a comma, but that is not. | A Facility corresponds to a physical shop floor location, which can be local or remote. A Checklist instance is an active object that is created based on the checklist. | A Facility corresponds to a physical shop floor location, that can be local or remote. A Checklist instance is an active object, which is created based on the checklist. |
| **Everyday versus every day** | The word everyday refers to something that is common. When you want to refer to something that happens each day, use every day. | The release environments are updated every day. | The release environments are updated everyday. |
| **Parallelism** | In tables and lists the descriptions all start similarly. Using parallelism is important because it's grammatically correct and makes it easier to read the content. | A Facility is where you:<br/>- Plan your factory<br/>- House materials<br/>- Create workstations | A Facility is where you:<br/>- Plan your factory<br/>- House materials<br/>- Creating workstations |
| **Past participle as an adjective** | Try as much as possible to avoid using the past participle as an adjective right after the noun. Note that there are some fixed expressions that you do put the participle after the noun, such as Lessons learned. | Create your BOM by using the selected materials. | Create your BOM by using the materials selected. |
| **Be clear and accurate when using it.** | Using pronouns correctly is fundamental in any type of writing. | After you save the document, you can close it. The system is missing the information to change the configuration. For what it is used. | After you save the document, you can close. It's missing the information to change the configuration. For what is used. |
| **Apostrophe versus acute accent.** | English uses an apostrophe for contractions and not the acute accent. The apostrophe is in the same key as the question mark. | I'm validating the Material tracking. We've prepared various e-Learning courses for you. | I´m validating the Material tracking. We´ve prepared various e-Learning courses for you. |
| **Out-of-the-box versus out of the box.** | Use out-of-the-box (with hyphens) when this expression is used as a qualifier. Otherwise, use out of the box (without hyphens). | Critical Manufacturing has many out-of-the-box features. These features are provided out of the box. | Critical Manufacturing has many out of the box features. These features are provided out-of-the-box. |

## Formatting

### Bold

Use bold only in the cases below.

#### Critical Manufacturing MES Entities

In technical documents, all Critical Manufacturing MES entities should be displayed in bold. However, if there is the possibility of displaying a hyperlink to a definition page that allows direct navigation, this hyperlink overrides the bold emphasis.

#### GUI Elements

When referring to the Critical Manufacturing MES GUI elements, use bold for:

1. Main menus
2. Buttons
3. Views
4. Sections

#### Tables

Bold is used in key entries and headings.

### Italic

Italic should be used sparingly and bold is the preferred method for emphasis in Critical Manufacturing MES technical documents. A notable exception is identifying mathematical constants and variables.

### Time

Prefer the 12-hour clock. Be sure to write AM or PM.

Avoid abbreviating the units of time for seconds, minutes and hours when in sentences. But when you must abbreviate for lack of space, use the following for both singular and plural:

| Singular | Plural  | Abbreviation |
| -------- | ------- | :----------: |
| hour     | hours   | hr           |
| minute   | minutes | min          |
| second   | seconds | sec          |

For the sake of consistency avoid using:

- 7:30 a.m.
- 19:30
- 10 p.m.
- 2 mins
- 2min

### Numbers

In general, spell out small numbers, ranging from zero to nine. But if you have space limitations, such as in tables, it is OK to use numerals. Larger numbers, above nine, should be written as numerals.

Furthermore, always use numerals for decimals, and for small numbers, with:

- Version, patch numbers:
    - Critical Manufacturing MES version 6.1 is no longer in operation.
    - Critical Manufacturing MES version 8.3 patch 1 was released.
- Quantities (such as disk space, memory, queries, choices):
    - You have 7GB of memory on your disk.
    - You have 5 options to choose from.
    - The exception is when mixing uses of numbers:
        - Enter two 3s.
- Percentages:
    - You have 33 percent of your system backed up.
- Page numbers:
    - For more information, go to page 54.
- Chapter numbers:
    - For more information, see chapter 14.

When referring to a range of numbers, it is common to use a hyphen to indicate the range. As such, you avoid the use of **from** and **between**.

### Tables

Ensure that all tables have captions, formatted as "Table X: Description". If they don't exist, generate them.

## Grammar

Having a set of grammar, punctuation, and capitalization guidelines is very important to produce clear and consistent writing. Use the following to help you with some of the most relevant grammar topics.

| **Topic** | **Guideline** | **Do** | **Don't** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Abbreviations (e.g., i.e., etc.)** | Avoid Latin abbreviations for common English phrases. - e.g. means for example<br/>- i.e. means that is/in other words | The conversion factor can be used in both directions (for example, from Wafers-to-Dies and Dies-to-Wafers). Use this operation to create a shift plan, that is, a step-by-step list of actions. The command lines must be adapted to match your operating system, directory locations, versions, and others. | The conversion factor can be used in both directions (e.g., from Wafers-to-Dies and Dies-to-Wafers). Use this operation to create a shift plan (i.e., a step-by-step list of actions). The command lines must be adapted to match your operating system, directory locations, versions, etc. |
| **Acronyms** | Use acronyms as they save space and avoid redundancy. The first time you mention an acronym in a document, spell it out and specify it in parentheses. If the acronym is well-known, you may decide not to spell it out. | Critical Manufacturing MES requires Active Directory (AD) or a Central Authentication Service (CAS) for user identification and authentication. The implementation of the component is done in the HTML tab. | Critical Manufacturing MES requires AD or a CAS for user identification and authentication. The implementation of the component is done in the HyperText Markup Language tab. |
| **Articles** | Try to use a definite (the) or indefinite (a/an) article instead of omitting it. Using articles eases the flow of the text. | The playground is where you can see the components in action. | Playground is where you can see components in action. |
| **Contractions** | Use common contractions, such as it's, you're, that's, and don't, but avoid ambiguous or awkward contractions, such as there'd, it'll, and they'd. | Through Business Data, it's possible to access Service. For main entities this means a default behavior and that's why the same partition function can't be shared among these. | Upon opening the wizard, it'll be in the top-left corner. When the report is available, there'd be a list of materials used. |
| **Possessive case/of structure** | Both are correct but sometimes one structure reads more naturally than the other. In any case, using the of structure instead of the possessive 's avoids any type of misunderstanding. Avoid using the possessive form of trademarks and product, service, or feature names. | The properties of the Checklist. Critical Manufacturing MES is quality software. The features of IoT Data Platform Asset Manager allow customers to create a directory of all their assets. | The Checklist's properties. Critical Manufacturing's MES is quality software. IoT Data Platform Asset Manager's features allows customers to create a directory of all their assets. |
| **Present tense** | Use the present tense as it is easier to read and avoid using the future tense, unless strictly necessary. With instructions use the imperative form (also known as command) to tell readers exactly what to do. | The Material is ready to track in. Clear the selected items to continue. | The Material will be ready to track in. If you intend to continue, you should think of clearing the selected items. |

## Language and Style

### Language

The documentation of Critical Manufacturing is written in English, U.S. variant (en-US).

### Voice and Tone

Use a consistent voice and tone throughout the document.

Keep the tone natural, friendly, simple, and professional.

Keep the message clear, concise, and relevant to the reader.

Prefer active voice whenever possible. It is usually easier to understand and shorter.

### Point of View

Use second or third person, depending on what reads most clearly.

Prefer **You** over **The user** in instructional content.

First person plural can be used when it improves the flow of explanatory content.

### Style

Keep the message clear, brief, and consistent. If you mean the same thing, use the same word.

| **Guideline** | **Why** | **Do** | **Don't** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Use Critical Manufacturing to refer to the company and Critical Manufacturing MES to refer to the software.** | You want your reader to know exactly what you are referring to consistently and correctly. | Critical Manufacturing has its headquarters in Maia, Portugal. Critical Manufacturing MES can be extended and customized. | Critical Manufacturing MES has its headquarters in Maia, Portugal. Critical Manufacturing can be extended and customized. |

## Punctuation

Punctuation is very important for the clear understanding of the reader and every sentence, unless it is a title or heading, requires a period at the end even if it contains only a few words.

### Commas and periods

Remember that too much punctuation makes the sentence more complex. Hence, if you have more than two commas in a sentence, consider revising it.

However, in a list of three or more items, you should use a comma before the conjunction (known as the Oxford comma) to avoid any confusion and ambiguity.

In relation to exclamation and question marks, you should avoid them unless they are totally necessary.

Semicolons are a good way to shorten your sentences. And although they should be used sparingly, they are useful to link two separate clauses that are similar in thought.

Nevertheless, when you have various items or concepts to describe, do not use semicolons and break the items into a list. If you are using bullets (unordered list), you should use a period at the end of each entry, unless all of them are short (two or three words maximum).

### Lists

Numbered lists should only be used when describing sequential steps (ordered list), that is, when a step depends on the execution of the previous step.

If you need to describe a concept in a list entry, use a dash to separate the concept and the definition. Also, the letter after the dash should be lowercase and the concept can be in bold.

As to the use of parentheses, use them sparingly. Nonetheless, if the information is relevant, use another more appropriate element such as a visual convention (see the list in [Images](images.md)). If the information is not important, you may need to consider whether it is worth adding at all.

### Dashes

- Never use double dashes (--) or em dashes (—), always use a single dash (-) when separating text.

## Spelling

Use U.S. English spelling consistently.

## Section-Specific Audience and Style Guidelines

Use this section to calibrate writing depth, vocabulary, and tone when creating or reviewing content in a specific part of the documentation. Each entry identifies who actually reads that section in the field, what they expect, and what rules to apply.

### Practical Use

Use this section when deciding how much to explain, what terms to define, and what tone to use while creating or reviewing documentation. If a page spans multiple audiences, identify the user who must act on the page and write for that user first. Then add technical depth through structured notes, warnings, examples, links, or reference tables.

### General Cross-Section Rules

Use the repository style guide as the baseline for all sections: preserve product terminology, use exact Critical Manufacturing MES GUI labels, use bold for GUI elements and MES entities where appropriate, define acronyms on first use unless they are clearly standard for the section audience, and prefer concise present-tense writing.

Tune the writing depth to the reader:

- IT, infrastructure, and operations sections can assume high familiarity with deployment platforms, databases, identity systems, observability, and security concepts.
- OT, automation, and equipment-integration sections can assume familiarity with factory automation, protocols, equipment behavior, and IT/OT integration.
- Manufacturing, maintenance, quality, planning, and logistics sections can assume domain knowledge but should not assume deep software, database, or infrastructure knowledge.
- Tutorial content should use imperative, numbered steps and should avoid long conceptual explanations inside task flows.
- Reference content should be scannable, table-driven where useful, and precise about states, properties, permissions, prerequisites, limits, and exceptions.

When the audience is mixed, write for the least technical reader who must successfully complete the task, and move advanced details into notes, warnings, examples, or cross-references.

### Introduction

#### Target audience composition

This is the broadest audience in the documentation. It includes technical and non-technical readers, but it should assume low prior familiarity with Critical Manufacturing MES and avoid relying on MES, IT, or manufacturing jargon without context.

#### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Define product-specific terms and acronyms on first use. Prefer full product and section names in headings. Avoid unexplained deployment, automation, or database terms.
- Voice: Welcoming, authoritative, and orienting. Use a calm explanatory voice rather than procedural urgency.
- Technical familiarity: Low. Write for intelligent readers who may be new to MES software and to the Critical Manufacturing documentation set.

### System Requirements

#### Target audience composition

The audience is highly technical and infrastructure-focused. Manufacturing floor users are not the primary readers. Readers are often using this section before installation to confirm feasibility, sizing, supportability, and operational constraints.

#### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use infrastructure-native vocabulary precisely. Terms such as Kubernetes, SQL Server, ClickHouse, Kafka, RabbitMQ, S3, latency, bandwidth, high availability, and storage area network do not need basic definitions for this audience. Define MES-specific components on first use.
- Voice: Precise, factual, and constraint-driven. Prefer tables, requirement lists, compatibility notes, and warnings over narrative explanation.
- Technical familiarity: High. Assume readers can interpret hardware sizing, database, networking, and deployment terminology, but do not assume they know Critical Manufacturing-specific component names.

### Installation Guide

#### Target audience composition

The audience is technical, hands-on, and task-driven. Readers may not be MES experts, but they are expected to understand enterprise software deployment, cloud or container platforms, identity and security configuration, and database setup.

#### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use deployment and infrastructure terms accurately, including Kubernetes, EKS, OpenShift, SQL Server, Kafka, RabbitMQ, S3, ingress, storage, deployment package, Infrastructure Agent, and DevOps Center. Bold exact GUI labels, screens, buttons, and MES entities.
- Voice: Procedural, imperative, and unambiguous. Use numbered steps for actions, prerequisites before the steps they affect, and warnings for destructive or non-reversible actions.
- Technical familiarity: High to very high. Do not explain common infrastructure concepts, but explain Critical Manufacturing-specific setup concepts, tools, screens, and package behavior.

### User Guide

#### Target audience composition

This is the most diverse section. Technical familiarity varies from low for general UI and shop-floor execution topics to very high for data platform, automation, and administration topics. The writing must adapt by subsection.

#### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use exact product terminology and bold MES entities and GUI elements according to the style guide. Define acronyms at first use unless they are standard for the subsection audience.
- Voice: Reference-oriented but practical. Use procedural wording when describing UI actions and concise explanatory wording when describing concepts or entity behavior.
- Technical familiarity: Variable. Each subsection should set its own technical baseline, as described in the User Guide subsection entries below.

### Operation Guide

#### Target audience composition

The section has a split audience. System Administration content is infrastructure- and runtime-focused. Application Administration content is MES configuration-focused. Readers usually arrive with an operational problem, change request, audit question, or troubleshooting need.

#### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: For system administration, use container, database, identity, observability, and security terminology precisely. For application administration, use MES configuration terms such as licenses, localized messages, message bus, master data packages, security, system configuration entries, and UNS export integration.
- Voice: Authoritative and operational. Use clear procedures, configuration references, and troubleshooting structures. For troubleshooting, prefer a Symptom, Cause, Resolution pattern when practical.
- Technical familiarity: High for application administration and very high for system administration. Avoid basic explanations of enterprise operations concepts, but clarify product-specific behavior and cross-layer dependencies.

### Tutorials

#### Target audience composition

The audience usually has strong domain knowledge and mixed software familiarity. Readers are learning by doing, often in a training or enablement setting. Some tutorials are basic UI how-tos, while others target advanced module specialists.

#### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use manufacturing domain terms such as Material, Resource, Step, Flow, Recipe, BOM, Production Order, Transfer Order, Schedule, Inspection, and SPC with confidence. Define system-specific or advanced terms when they first appear in a tutorial.
- Voice: Instructional, task-focused, and supportive. Use numbered steps, exact GUI labels, screenshots where possible, and short outcomes after major tasks.
- Technical familiarity: Low to medium by default, increasing only for specialized tutorials such as automation, IoT Data Platform, or equipment integration. Avoid infrastructure vocabulary unless the tutorial explicitly requires it.

### User Guide Subsections

The following entries give writing guidance for each of the eleven subsections within the User Guide. Apply these rules in addition to the top-level User Guide rules above.

#### General Concepts

##### Target audience composition

The audience is broad and low-assumption. Readers may have manufacturing expertise but limited familiarity with Critical Manufacturing MES UI conventions.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use plain UI vocabulary such as page, grid, view, entity, revision, version, template, and shortcut. Define MES-specific patterns before relying on them.
- Voice: Accessible, concise, and explanatory. Use second person for actions and keep examples simple.
- Technical familiarity: Low. Avoid module jargon unless it is needed as a short example.

#### Administration

##### Target audience composition

The audience is moderately to highly technical in MES terms, but not necessarily infrastructure-focused. Readers often manage permissions, configuration, master data packages, or runtime application behavior.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use MES administration vocabulary precisely, including Security Feature, Role, License, State Model, Query, Timer, Master Data Package, DEE Action, Event Rule, UI Page, Table, and Message Bus. Bold exact GUI elements and MES entities.
- Voice: Dense, factual, and reference-oriented. Prioritize prerequisites, field meanings, configuration effects, and permission requirements.
- Technical familiarity: Medium-high. Explain product-specific behavior, but do not over-explain standard administration concepts.

#### Low Code

##### Target audience composition

The audience is comfortable with structured logic, expressions, configuration, and workflow concepts, but may not be professional software developers.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use low-code terms consistently, including UI Page, Automation Workflow, Business Workflow, DEE Action, Event Rule, Entity Type, Query, Table, and Security Feature. Clearly distinguish low-code configuration from developer-portal extension work.
- Voice: Conceptual plus procedural. Briefly explain what each tool is for, then give concrete configuration guidance.
- Technical familiarity: Medium-high. Readers can handle logic and configuration vocabulary, but code-level assumptions should be avoided unless the page explicitly covers code expressions or scripts.

#### Business Data

##### Target audience composition

The audience has medium technical familiarity and strong manufacturing domain knowledge. Readers usually need precise reference information about entities such as Material, Resource, Recipe, Product, BOM, Flow, Step, Production Order, Schedule, Qualification, or Transfer Order.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use entity names exactly and consistently. Bold MES entities according to the style guide. Keep property names, state names, and action names aligned with the UI.
- Voice: Lookup-oriented and structured. Prefer tables, state descriptions, relationship summaries, and concise action explanations.
- Technical familiarity: Medium. Assume MES and manufacturing vocabulary, but avoid database or infrastructure terminology unless it directly affects entity behavior.

#### Manufacturing

##### Target audience composition

The audience may have strong practical manufacturing knowledge but limited software or technical documentation familiarity. Content should support quick task completion and recognition of UI elements.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use factory execution vocabulary such as Material, Resource, Step, Work Order, WIP, track in, track out, barcode, FabExplorer, FabLive, and My Workgroup. Avoid infrastructure and database terminology.
- Voice: Task-oriented, direct, and visually supported. Keep sentences short and action-focused.
- Technical familiarity: Low. Explain MES-specific UI behavior clearly and avoid assuming configuration knowledge.

#### Maintenance

##### Target audience composition

The audience has maintenance and manufacturing domain knowledge with low to medium software familiarity. Some readers execute tasks; others configure or supervise the process.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use maintenance vocabulary such as Maintenance Activity Order, MAO, Maintenance Plan, Maintenance Schedule, preventive maintenance, corrective maintenance, resource, and due date. Define MAO on first use in each standalone page.
- Voice: Procedural and practical. Prioritize what the technician or supervisor must do next.
- Technical familiarity: Low to medium. Explain MES-specific maintenance behavior, but do not explain general maintenance concepts.

#### Quality

##### Target audience composition

The audience has medium technical familiarity and strong quality or compliance domain knowledge. Accuracy, traceability, and terminology consistency matter more than conversational simplicity.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use quality terms precisely, including SPC, control chart, EFC, Inspection Order, Material Hold, Qualification, Certification, Protocol Instance, Change Set, and experiment. Define specialized acronyms on first use unless the page is already deep within that feature.
- Voice: Precise, controlled, and audit-friendly. Avoid vague approximations and clearly distinguish configuration, execution, review, and approval activities.
- Technical familiarity: Medium. Assume quality domain expertise, but explain how MES implements the quality process.

#### Planning and Logistics

##### Target audience composition

The audience has medium technical familiarity and strong production planning, logistics, or warehouse domain knowledge. Readers need both operational guidance and a clear understanding of system constraints.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use planning and logistics vocabulary such as Schedule, Schedule Scenario, Production Order, Transfer Requirement, Transfer Order, Pick List, Periodic Inventory, Package, Material Transfer, warehouse, and APS where relevant.
- Voice: Practical, structured, and process-oriented. Make dependencies, status meanings, and ownership clear.
- Technical familiarity: Medium. Assume domain knowledge, but explain MES-specific scheduling, transfer, and permission behavior.

#### Business Intelligence

##### Target audience composition

The audience has medium-high technical familiarity. Readers know analytics concepts and need to understand the MES reporting model, data freshness, aggregation behavior, and access patterns.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use BI terms such as report, Analytics View, ODS, DWH, OLAP, cube, aggregation, SQL Server Reporting Services, dimension, measure, and R integration. Spell out ODS and DWH on first use in standalone pages.
- Voice: Technical reference with operational clarity. Explain data latency, aggregation, and filter behavior precisely.
- Technical familiarity: Medium-high. Do not explain general BI concepts, but clarify MES-specific data architecture and permissions.

#### Automation

##### Target audience composition

The audience is highly technical and sits between IT and OT. Readers are expected to understand equipment integration concepts and may also need software configuration detail.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use automation vocabulary precisely, including Connect IoT, Automation Controller, Automation Driver Definition, Automation Protocol, Automation Workflow, Automation Manager, task, converter, event, driver, protocol, PLC, and specific communication protocol names when relevant.
- Voice: Technical, explicit, and option-aware. Configuration paths, defaults, runtime effects, and monitoring signals must be clear.
- Technical familiarity: High. Assume automation and IT/OT integration knowledge, but explain Critical Manufacturing-specific automation object relationships.

#### Data Platform

##### Target audience composition

This is one of the most technically advanced User Guide subsections. Readers are expected to understand data architecture, event ingestion, analytics access, and possibly machine learning lifecycle concepts.

##### Prescriptive style rules

- Terminology: Use data engineering terms accurately, including Data Platform, Canonical Data Model, CDM, ISA-95, OData, IoT Event Definition, IoT Schema, Data Set, Data Platform Workflow, ML Model, Grafana, Cube.dev, event ingestion, materialization, enrichment, and asset.
- Voice: Technical reference with enough conceptual context to support architecture decisions. Mark preview features clearly.
- Technical familiarity: High to very high. Assume data and platform knowledge, but explain product-specific data objects, lifecycle states, and access points.
